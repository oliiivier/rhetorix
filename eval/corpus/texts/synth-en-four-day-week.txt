The four-day week is no longer a fringe idea. Companies, unions and governments are all looking at it, and the debate is heating up.

Between 2015 and 2019, Iceland ran trials of shorter working hours involving about 2,500 public sector workers. Researchers reported that productivity was maintained or improved in most workplaces, and that participants felt less stressed.

My cousin's startup switched to a four-day week last spring and doubled its revenue within six months. That is proof that it works for every kind of business.

Experts agree that productivity always rises when working hours fall. As a famous actor put it in a recent interview, "nobody needs a fifth day of work."

The choice is clear. Either we adopt the four-day week, or we accept burnout as the price of having a job.

Critics like to say the idea is unrealistic, but America has already shown the way: the United States has had a legal 32-hour work week since 1938.

None of this means the transition will be painless. Hospitals, schools and other services that run every day would need more staff to cover the same hours, and nobody has yet worked out who would pay for them. Those questions deserve careful answers before any national law is passed.
